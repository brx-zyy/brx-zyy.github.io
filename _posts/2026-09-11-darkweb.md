## Challenge Scenario

Last month, our efforts led to the successful dismantling of a major drug trafficking network operating within the UK through the TOR network. The network's primary marketplace was taken down, significantly disrupting their operations and preventing further illicit substances from reaching our streets. However, critical intelligence suggests that one of the masterminds behind this network evaded capture and continued their illegal activities. They have established a platform to "tell their stories" of criminal exploits, serving as a covert hub for their operations. Your mission is to bring this individual to justice. This will not be easy, but we are confident in your expertise and ability to achieve this mission. Your success is pivotal to dismantling this criminal enterprise and safeguarding the public.

**Objectives:**

1. Gain access to the site. Visit the URL below, right-click and select "Inspect Element" when presented with a login screen, select the Console tab, and enter the command `generateUserCredentials()`. Decode the answer, and you're good to go.
2. Find evidence that the individual(s) is involved in drug trafficking.
3. Find any information about the locations in which these criminals congregate.

**Site:** `http://panznjcktrpezyln5frnjxf5gv4xoyi7wvd3ykeu6bejxvbynhfpasqd.onion`

### 1. Getting past the login

The onion site's login page does not actually check credentials against a backend. Instead it ships a client side function that generates them for you, which is a common pattern in training environments where the "vulnerability" is really just an invitation to open the developer tools.

![Alt text describing the image](/assets/2.png)

Right clicking the page and choosing **Inspect**, then switching to the **Console** tab, lets you call the function directly:

```shell
generateUserCredentials()
```
![Alt text describing the image](/assets/3.png)

This returns a Base64 blob:

```shell
Base64 Encoded Credentials: S0Y3eWJ1RDE6QWx5aGZvdDBWOVZJV202Vw==
```

Base64 is not encryption, it is just an encoding scheme for representing binary data as text, so decoding it is trivial:

```shell
echo 'S0Y3eWJ1RDE6QWx5aGZvdDBWOVZJV202Vw==' | base64 -d
KF7ybuD1:Alyhfot0V9VIWm6W
```

That colon separated string is a username and password pair. Dropping `KF7ybuD1` and `Alyhfot0V9VIWm6W` into the login form gets you onto the dashboard, which greets you with "Welcome to the Dark Side" and a handful of posts to dig through.

![Alt text describing the image](/assets/5.png)

### 2. Decoding the pinned post titles

The three featured posts on the dashboard have their titles rendered as space separated hex bytes instead of plain text, which is a cheap but effective way to keep casual scrapers and search engines from indexing the content directly.

Each pair of hex digits is one ASCII byte, so feeding the string through `xxd` with the reverse and plain flags turns it straight back into text:

```shell
echo "44 72 75 67 73" | xxd -r -p
Drugs

echo "50 6C 65 61 73 75 72 65" | xxd -r -p
Pleasure

echo "44 72 6F 70 73" | xxd -r -p
Drops
```
![Alt text describing the image](/assets/0.png)

The captions underneath each image use the same encoding and decode out to lines like "We have Percocet, Codeine, and Yercs," "Enjoy your vices with servants at your disposal," and "See your demons with these galaxy eye-drops." Between the titles and captions it is clear this dashboard is advertising narcotics under a thin layer of slang.

### 3. Following the link to Midnite

Scrolling down to the site's message board turns up a short exchange between users, and one of them, Basilisk95, drops a hyperlink with the message "Lol, guys. Have you seen this?" Another user replies that the linked group is amateur and running their operation on the clear web, which is a mistake the operators of Midnite apparently made.

![Alt text describing the image](/assets/6.png)

Following that link lands on a second onion service, `btlomidnite.s3-website.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com`, hosted for a self-described hacking collective called Midnite. Despite the branding as a shadowy operation, the site is sitting on an unprotected AWS S3 static website endpoint, openly advertising DDoS-for-hire tiers and a marketplace for stolen crypto wallet private keys. That is the second illegal platform the capstone is asking you to identify, and its hosting choice is a solid lead for anyone trying to track the operators down through AWS abuse reporting.

### 4. Pulling the leaked transaction log

![Alt text describing the image](/assets/8.png)

Back on the original dashboard, the "Recent Transactions" section lists four purchases, and the fourth one, a "$7,000" custom Vice Bundle, is hyperlinked. That link resolves to a PDF sitting in the site's static file directory:

```shell
http://panznjcktrpezyln5frnjxf5gv4xoyi7wvd3ykeu6bejxvbynhfpasqd.onion/static/Transaction Log 098.pdf
```

Whoever built this site forgot that linking a file does not make it private. The PDF is a full internal transaction record, and it names names:

- Customer: Frank Castle
- Contact: frankcastle2093@gmail.com
- Amount: $7,000.00, paid by credit card
- Purpose of transaction: "Hookers and Drugs"
- Notes: a complaint about a previous order's makeup and toe polish being wrong

![Alt text describing the image](/assets/9.png)


This is the kind of sloppy operational security that actually gets people caught. A marketplace operator leaving a customer's real name and personal Gmail address sitting in a publicly linked PDF is a gift to any investigator.

### 5. Solving PJ's party riddle

The dashboard's pinned "Best Party Ever" post is from a user who identifies himself as PJ, the site owner, inviting people to a party on February 14 and promising drugs, minors-unsafe chaos, and paid off police. He also mentions the location is hidden inside the image itself, which is the tell for steganography.

Downloading the photo and running it through a steganography decoder confirms it is using LSB (least significant bit) embedding, where secret data is smuggled into the lowest order bits of each pixel's color channels without visibly changing the image. Tools like `zsteg` are built exactly for pulling this kind of payload out of PNGs:

![Alt text describing the image](/assets/10.png)

```shell
zsteg baily1.png
```
![Alt text describing the image](/assets/11.png)

One of the extracted text channels reveals the hidden message:

```shell
Where the red dragon flies and the Severn meets the sea. Find the capital of Cymru, where your next adventure will be.
```

That is a riddle pointing at Wales. The red dragon is the national symbol on the Welsh flag, the River Severn empties into the Bristol Channel along the Welsh-English border, and Cymru is the Welsh name for Wales. The capital of Wales is Cardiff, which gives the location. Combined with the February 14 date from the post text, that is enough to hand law enforcement a time and place to intercept PJ's party.

### 6. Decoding the car parts contact email

One more post on the dashboard advertises "cheap stolen car parts" alongside a "complementary drug package," with a contact string given as a long run of hex pairs instead of plain text, the same trick used on the post titles earlier.

Running it through the same hex decode gets the poster's real contact email:

![Alt text describing the image](/assets/13.png)

```shell
echo '74 77 69 7A 7A 65 72 69 63 68 61 72 64 40 67 6D 61 69 6C 2E 63 6F 6D' | xxd -r -pRR
twizzerichard@gmail.com
```

Another operator who thought hex encoding a Gmail address counted as operational security.
